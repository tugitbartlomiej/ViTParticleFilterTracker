## 1. Neural population dynamics: jPCA and its critiques, dPCA, LFADS-type latent models, cross-session alignment (Gallego et al. 2020)

### Takeaway
jPCA picks the plane that maximizes *fitted rotation*. Rotation then appears whenever responses are smooth, latency-shifted or ordered in sequence, whether or not the system is autonomous. Simple tuning, sequences, travelling waves, feedback inputs and even networks with no recurrence have all produced significant jPCA rotations. Rotation counts as evidence of dynamics only against a null that keeps the simpler structure: TME surrogates, covariance-matched permutations, or residual (trial-to-trial) dynamics. Dynamics are compared across sessions by aligning latent *trajectories* (CCA), not by comparing subspaces.

### Cited Findings
**jPCA (Churchland et al. 2012, Nature 487:51)**
- Procedure: soft-normalize each neuron, subtract each neuron's cross-condition mean at each time point, keep the top **6 PCs**, then fit dX/dt = M X twice: unconstrained, and with M_skew = −M_skewᵀ. The first jPCA plane is the real plane of the complex eigenvector pair of M_skew with the largest |imaginary eigenvalue|. All six jPCs span the same space as the six PCs — [Churchland et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11129)
- Numbers: the first jPCA plane captured on average **28%** of total variance (PC2+PC3 captured 29%). Two or three rotational planes together captured **50–70%**. Rotations lasted only **~1–1.5 cycles**, at frequencies up to ~**2.5 Hz**. Fitted angular frequencies were 8.8 vs 9.8 rad/s (monkey A) and 12.2 vs 13.5 rad/s (monkey B) for fast vs slow reaches. The paper reports only the ratio R²(M_skew)/R²(M_best) (Fig. 6b, "nearly as good"), not absolute R² — [Churchland et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11129)
- The authors' controls: shuffles, velocity-tuned and complex-kinematic simulated populations, and EMG. None gave consistent rotations. They interpreted the rotations as the preparatory state setting the initial condition of a dynamical system — [Churchland et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature11129)

**Critiques and controls**
- *Michaels, Dann & Scherberger 2016* (PLoS Comput Biol): a purely representational velocity-tuning model with **variable neuron–kinematic latencies** produces jPCA rotations. Its rotational goodness-of-fit ratio RGR = R²_skew/R²_M was **0.79** (circularity 0.72; 16% of variance in plane 1), against **0.97** for a dynamical model. Rotation strength "rose rapidly when latency SD exceeded movement-activity SD" (72 vs 56 ms in their example). Their covariance-matched permutation test (CMPT) separated the two: the representational model was not significant (**p = 0.71**), the dynamical model was (**p < 0.001**). Real PMd/M1 data were significant (p < 0.001). Caveat: CMPT needs **>30 neurons and >8 conditions** — [Michaels et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005175)
- *Elsayed & Cunningham 2017* (Nat Neurosci 20:1310): proposed surrogate populations (Corrected Fisher Randomization, and **Tensor Maximum Entropy, TME**) that preserve the mean and the covariances across **time, neurons and conditions** but are otherwise random. The question is whether population structure is "an expected byproduct" of these primary features. Results: in motor cortex, a low-dimensional linear-dynamical-system fit had R² significantly above every surrogate type (**P < 0.001**). Surrogates that preserve all three covariances (surrogate-TNC) already reach a much higher R² than the time-only or time+neuron surrogates, so tuning alone creates some apparent dynamics. In PFC, the dPCA decision readout exceeded the null while the stimulus readout did **not** (consistent with single-neuron tuning). The paper tests an LDS fit, not jPCA specifically — [Elsayed & Cunningham 2017, PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5577566)
- *Lebedev et al. 2019* (Sci Rep 9:17616): reanalysis of Churchland's 218 neurons × 108 conditions. Rotations appear when (1) neurons' firing peaks follow a temporal sequence and (2) the sequence is consistent across conditions. Simulated Gaussian PETHs with no directional tuning, peak-ordered and given onset differences between condition groups, gave jPCA circles. Without temporal spread they did not. Rotations in PC traces survived per-neuron condition shuffling. jPCA's cross-condition mean subtraction made the shuffled data ill-conditioned; removing it restored circles. Linear regression could fit circles and ∞-shaped Lissajous curves to the data — [Lebedev et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6908571/)
- *Kuzmina, Kriukov & Lebedev 2024* (Sci Rep): across several datasets, rotational dynamics were "consistently accounted for by a traveling wave pattern". They introduced a complex-valued "gyration number" to quantify rotation strength — [Kuzmina et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-53907-2)
- *Kalidindi et al. 2021* (eLife): RNN controllers of a model arm with sensory feedback. Networks **with recurrent connections removed** still gave significant skew-symmetric fits: R² = 0.43 (posture, p = 0.02 vs TME) and 0.46 (reach, p = 0.01). The first two jPCA planes held 82–92% of output-layer variance. Sensory-feedback input layers rotated (top two planes 74% variance; R²_skew 0.51 vs TME median 0.29). Somatosensory cortex also rotated (R²_skew 0.49, p < 0.001). Conclusion: rotations "may reflect internal dynamics, external inputs or any combination". TME was unreliable below ~30 signals — [Kalidindi et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8691841/)
- *Shinn 2023* (PNAS), "Phantom oscillations in PCA": PCA of data that vary smoothly along a continuum (time or space), or that contain **shifts/latency variability**, yields oscillatory components. They "can explain substantial variance and survive ordinary cross-validation", because independent samples share the same smoothness. Shifts produce derivative-shaped PCs (x(t−τ) ≈ f − τ f′ + …). The paper demonstrates this in LIP neural data (54 neurons) and notes that it has been reported with jPCA and dPCA. Diagnostics: (i) a power-spectrum peak (phantoms show 1/f-like spectra); (ii) a covariance matrix with one diagonal stripe (phantom) vs several stripes (genuine); (iii) PC frequency rising monotonically with PC index (phantom); (iv) PC scores related to estimated latency (shift-driven) — [Shinn 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2311420120)
- *Proix, Perich & Milekovic 2022* [preprint]: oscillatory trajectories after dimensionality reduction "arise as a consequence of the horseshoe effect" for signals that vary continuously in time, "regardless of whether latent oscillatory dynamical systems are present" — [Proix et al. 2022](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/b0660b69e1d77a4c5f09b3689c25f26a33ebc20a)
- *Sabatini & Kaufman 2024* (Nat Commun): the rotation planes **differ across reaches**, and the plane depends on where activity sits in state space. A "location-dependent rotations" model fits nearly all motor-cortex activity. A single global jPCA plane therefore misrepresents dynamics that are only locally rotational — [Sabatini & Kaufman 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-51308-7)
- *Galgali, Sahani & Mante 2023* (Nat Neurosci 26:326): they estimate local linear dynamics from **residuals** (trial-to-trial deviations around condition-averaged trajectories) instead of from the averages. In PFC, recurrent dynamics were time-dependent but stable, and the "pronounced rotational structure in PFC trajectories during saccades is driven by inputs from upstream areas" — [Galgali et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01230-2)
- *Rutten et al. 2020* (NeurIPS): standard GP priors "do not distinguish between underlying dynamical processes and other forms of temporal autocorrelation". **Temporal non-reversibility** is "a universal signature of autonomous dynamical systems whose state trajectories follow consistent flow fields". They introduce non-reversible kernels (GPFADS) that recover ground-truth phase portraits — [Rutten et al. 2020](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/5cf6abc9a1ec5e0488d55b94fa70b270b0c6edb5)

**Other subspace choices**
- *dPCA (Kobak et al. 2016, eLife)*: finds separate encoder/decoder axes that demix variance by task parameter (stimulus, decision, time). It targets *labelled variance*, not dynamics, and its axes need not be orthogonal (†) — [Kobak et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.10989). Elsayed & Cunningham showed that a dPCA stimulus readout can be fully explained by the TME null — [PMC5577566](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5577566)
- *"Hypothesis-guided" dimensionality reduction (Lara, Cunningham & Churchland 2018)*: "linear population dynamics, dominated by rotations, are prominent in motor cortex but largely absent in SMA", even though single-neuron responses look similar — [Lara et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-018-05146-z)
- *Dynamical Components Analysis (Clark et al. 2019)*: a linear projection that maximizes **predictive information** (mutual information between past and future). It is designed because variance-based methods are "prone to extracting noise rather than meaningful dynamics" — [Clark et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09944)
- *FCCA (Kumar, Frank & Bouchard 2024)* [preprint]: PCA maximizes a measure of *feedforward* controllability, and FCCA finds the most *feedback*-controllable subspace. They prove that **non-normality of the dynamics determines how far the PCA and FCCA subspaces diverge** — [Kumar et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2408.05875)

**Latent dynamical models**
- *LFADS (Pandarinath et al. 2018, Nat Methods)*: a sequential autoencoder that infers single-trial latent trajectories and the initial conditions/inputs of an RNN generator (†) — [Pandarinath et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-018-0109-9)
- *Sedler, VerSteeg & Pandarinath 2022*: "neural variance alone does not uniquely determine a latent dynamical system". SAEs with RNN-based dynamics failed at the true latent dimensionality, and larger RNNs "relied upon dynamical features not present in the data". Neural-ODE dynamics recovered the true trajectories and **fixed-point structure** at the true dimensionality — [Sedler et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2212.03771)
- *Duncker & Sahani 2021* review latent-dynamics models and their limits — [Duncker & Sahani 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.conb.2021.10.014)

**Cross-session alignment (Gallego et al. 2020, Nat Neurosci 23:260)**
- Method: per session, PCA to an m-dimensional manifold (**m = 10 for M1, 15 for PMd, 8 for S1**; robust over 6–12), then CCA between the latent time series of two sessions, with trials matched by target. Normalized similarity is the across-session CC divided by a within-day upper bound. M1 example: **0.93 ± 0.03 aligned vs 0.38 ± 0.14 unaligned**. This stability held for up to ~2 years in M1, ~500 days in PMd and ~45 days in S1. Controls (TME surrogates without temporal covariance, movement-tuning simulations, static target clusters) all aligned worse. Neurons turned over and each day's manifold was estimated separately, so what stays stable is the *dynamics within* the session-specific subspace, not the subspace — [Gallego et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-019-0555-4)
- Alignment of latent dynamics (not raw neurons) is used to stabilize BCIs over time — [Karpowicz et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-59652-y)

### Inferences
- In our system, 600 tokens × frames × 26 checkpoints form a tensor of exactly the neuron × condition × time kind. The TME null (preserve covariance across tokens, across frames/images, across checkpoints) is a direct, ready-made control for any rotation or "vortex" seen in checkpoint trajectories. CMPT (per-token condition permutation that preserves token–token covariance) is the second control.
- Training trajectories are smooth by construction (small learning-rate steps, momentum/EMA) and tokens may reorganize in a consistent order (latency/sequence structure). Shinn's and Lebedev's mechanisms then predict **rotations and phantom oscillations in PCA/jPCA of checkpoint trajectories even with no rotational dynamics**. Before reading rotations, apply Shinn's three diagnostics: spectrum peak, number of covariance stripes, PC frequency vs PC index.
- Galgali's residual approach translates to our setting: estimate local dynamics from *deviations* (e.g., across images or across seeds at matched checkpoints), not from the mean trajectory. This separates input-driven rotation (the image/patch "inputs") from intrinsic field rotation.

### Gaps
- Absolute R²(M_skew) values for Churchland 2012 are not in the main text (only ratios). The exact dimensionality of Elsayed & Cunningham's motor-cortex LDS fit was not confirmed.
- No paper found that applies TME/CMPT to *training-checkpoint* trajectories of deep networks.

## 2. Fixed points and linearization: Sussillo & Barak 2013, line attractors (Maheswaranathan et al. 2019), FixedPointFinder; which subspaces show local dynamics

### Takeaway
The established workflow has four steps. (1) Find fixed or slow points in the **full** state space by minimizing the speed q(x) ∝ ‖F(x)‖². (2) Linearize there with the full Jacobian. (3) Read the mechanism from eigen-modes, using left eigenvectors for input selection and right eigenvectors for integration directions. (4) Only then **project** states and fixed points onto a low-dimensional basis, usually the top PCs of visited states, reporting how much of the fixed points' variance that basis captures. Fixed points are never searched for inside a 2D/3D slice. Linearization is accurate for one step (~10% error) but drifts over many steps.

### Cited Findings
- Sussillo & Barak 2013 introduced finding fixed points **and slow points** (minima of q(x) = ½‖F(x)‖² with q small but non-zero) in high-dimensional RNNs, then linearizing around them. Examples include the 3-bit flip-flop (8 stable attractors connected by saddles) and a sine-wave generator (†) — [Sussillo & Barak 2013](https://doi.org/10.1162/NECO_a_00409). A description of the method (minimize the squared norm of the vector field; slow point = small but non-zero velocity; stability from the Jacobian eigenvalues) is in [BrainPy docs](https://brainpy.readthedocs.io/en/latest/tutorial_analysis/highdim_analysis.html)
- FixedPointFinder is a TensorFlow toolbox for this procedure (Golub & Sussillo 2018, JOSS 3(31):1003, as cited in Maheswaranathan et al.) — [Golub & Sussillo 2018](https://doi.org/10.21105/joss.01003)
- *Maheswaranathan et al. 2019* (NeurIPS, sentiment LSTMs): q = (1/N)‖h − F(h,0)‖² minimized from ~**500** initial states sampled from visited states. Points with q < 10⁻⁸ count as fixed points. Visualization subspace: **PCA of hidden states visited on 1,000 test examples**, where the top 2–3 PCs explain ~**90%** of state variance (untrained networks are much higher-dimensional). The fixed points, found in full space, lie on a 1D manifold whose direction m aligns with the readout. Jacobians at the fixed points have nearly all |λ| < 1, with one |λ₁| ≈ 1 (τ₁ ~ 1000 tokens vs ~100 for modes 2–3). The input projection ℓ₁ᵀ J_inp x (left eigenvector) separates positive from negative words. The right eigenvector satisfies r₁ᵀm ≈ 1. The linearized model's **single-step relative error is ~10%**, but errors accumulate over a document — [Maheswaranathan et al. 2019a](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.10720)
- *Maheswaranathan et al. 2019b* ("Universality and individuality", NeurIPS; 2,880 networks per task): SVCCA/CKA geometry clusters by architecture and nonlinearity. **Fixed-point topology** (graphs of fixed points and transition probabilities, linearized eigen-modes) is much more similar across architectures. SVCCA can mislead: a trained tanh net correlated 0.72 with a trained ReLU net but 0.81/0.80 with *untrained* nets — [Maheswaranathan et al. 2019b](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.08549)
- *Smith, Linderman & Sussillo 2021* (JSLDS): named the practical problems of post-hoc linearization, namely "difficulty choosing which fixed point to expand around" and "error accumulation". They co-train an RNN with a switching linear system whose auxiliary network outputs a fixed point for every state — [Smith et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.01256)
- Data-driven approach: Galgali et al. estimate local (residual) dynamics from data instead of from a known model — [Galgali et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-022-01230-2)
- A flow-preserving 3D projection for visualization: Bartolovic, Gross & Günther 2020 optimize a projection operator by energy minimization so that **critical points and flow maps** of the high-dimensional system are preserved. They compare it against PCA, t-SNE and UMAP — [Bartolovic et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.13978)

### Inferences
- The app's finding that "the zero of the 3D-slice field lies ~37 cell radii from the true 192D zero" is expected from the geometry. Within a slice x = x₀ + Vy, the in-slice field Vᵀ F(x₀+Vy) vanishes wherever F is *orthogonal* to the slice: 3 equations in 3 unknowns, with the other 189 components of F unconstrained. Such "slice zeros" generically exist in any slice and carry no information about 192D equilibria. The literature's practice is the remedy: minimize ‖F‖² in 192D (from many token positions as seeds, as with Maheswaranathan's ~500 seeds), then project the solutions into the display subspace and report the fraction of their variance it captures.
- Good 3D display bases for local dynamics around a true equilibrium x*: (a) an orthonormal basis of an **invariant subspace** of J(x*) (real Schur vectors of the selected eigenvalues; see Q3), which makes the 3×3 compression reproduce those eigenvalues exactly; (b) top PCs of the token cloud, with fixed points projected afterwards; (c) the readout-relevant directions (here, e.g., the head/CLS direction), as the readout-aligned line attractor in Maheswaranathan.
- Checks to adopt: single-step linearization error along real trajectories (benchmark ~10%), and the speed q at reported "traps".

### Gaps
- I did not re-read Sussillo & Barak 2013 this session (no PDF in Undermind). The q(x) definition and slow-point concept are confirmed through secondary documentation.
- No source found that quantifies how often slice zeros mislocate full-space equilibria (our 37-radii observation seems novel as a measurement).

## 3. Non-normal dynamics, Schur decomposition, pseudospectra: why eigen-projections, Schur vectors and invariant subspaces matter for rotation vs strain

### Takeaway
For a non-normal Jacobian, eigenvalues alone describe neither transients nor what an arbitrary low-dimensional view shows. An orthogonal compression VᵀJV has eigenvalues (Ritz values) anywhere inside the **numerical range W(J)**, which can extend far beyond the spectrum. A slice can therefore show "repelling", "vortex" or "saddle" behaviour the full operator lacks. The Schur form J = Q(D + N)Qᵀ cleanly separates the normal (eigenvalue) part D from the non-normal feedforward/shear part N. Invariant-subspace bases (Schur vectors) are the projections that preserve local spectral type. In fluid turbulence, the non-normal part of the velocity-gradient tensor is as large as the normal part, and it carries rotation (enstrophy) even when all eigenvalues are real.

### Cited Findings
- **Ritz values sit inside the numerical range (Embree 2019, Syst Control Lett)**: for orthonormal V, σ(VᵀAV) ⊆ W(A), so Re θ ≤ ω(A), the numerical abscissa. If W(A) lies in the left half-plane (equivalently A + Aᵀ negative definite), every orthogonal-projection reduced model is stable. For stable **normal** A this always holds. Otherwise a stable A can have unstable compressions. The count bound: with μ₁ ≥ … ≥ μₙ the eigenvalues of H = (A+A*)/2 and M_j their running averages, a k-dimensional compression has at most p eigenvalues with Re ≥ 0, where p is the largest index with M_p ≥ 0. Conversely, if H has q positive eigenvalues there *exists* a q-dimensional subspace whose compression is fully unstable. Initial transient growth rate is (d/dt)‖e^{tA}‖ at t = 0 = ω(A). Example: a stable matrix with eigenvalues {−1,…,−8} whose Krylov reduced models of order k have eigenvalues {1,…,k}, i.e., every reduced mode unstable — [Embree 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sysconle.2018.11.010)
- **Schur decomposition of the velocity-gradient tensor (Keylock 2018, JFM)**: it separates normal (eigenvalue) from non-normal parts, as a supplement to rotation/strain. In DNS of isotropic turbulence, "the norm of the non-normal part of the tensor is of a similar magnitude to the normal part". "Where eigenvalues are all real, enstrophy arises from the non-normal term only." Non-normality is critical for explaining behaviour in vortical regions — [Keylock 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2018.344)
- **Classical 3D critical-point classification** (focus/node/saddle, stable/unstable, from the invariants P, Q, R of the velocity-gradient tensor; Chong, Perry & Cantwell 1990, Phys Fluids A 2:765) is the direct analogue of the app's vortex/saddle/attracting/repelling labels (†) — [Chong et al. 1990](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.857730)
- **Vortex criteria are frame-dependent (Haller 2005, JFM 525:1)**: "the most widely used definitions of a vortex are not objective". Q and λ₂ are only Galilean-invariant and identify different structures in mutually rotating frames (summary via secondary sources) — [Haller 2005](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022112004002526); [secondary: Haller-related review, ar5iv 1506.03384](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1506.03384)
- **Hidden feedforward structure in recurrent networks**: Goldman 2009 (Neuron 61:621) showed that persistent activity can be maintained "by a purely feedforward mechanism" in which activity passes through a chain of network states. This happens even in "recurrent networks that function in a feedforward manner", so memory duration is not set by eigenvalue time constants — [Goldman 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2674525/). Murphy & Miller 2009 (Neuron 61:635), "balanced amplification": selective amplification "without elongation of lifetime", arising because Dale's law produces "hidden feedforward connectivity between activity patterns" (difference pattern → sum pattern) — [Murphy & Miller 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2667957)
- Hennequin, Vogels & Gerstner 2012 (PRE 86:011909): any matrix is a unitary transform of (diagonal eigenvalues + strictly triangular part), and the triangular part makes the network effectively feedforward. Non-normal amplification is generic in random balanced networks — [Hennequin et al. 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1204.2945)
- Christodoulou, Vogels & Agnes 2022: work directly in the Schur basis (upper-triangular matrices) to set eigenspectrum and feedforward structure independently. Counter-intuitively, "shrinking the eigenspectrum's imaginary distribution leads to highly amplifying regimes". There is a trade-off between amplification and trajectory dimensionality — [Christodoulou et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1010365)
- Gudowska-Nowak et al. 2020: imposing E/I balance leaves the eigenvalues unchanged but "their stability dramatically decreases due to the strong nonorthogonality of associated eigenvectors". One must track eigenvector overlaps, not just eigenvalues — [Gudowska-Nowak et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1162/neco_a_01253)
- Reuter 2019 [preprint]: links transient growth to specific **2D invariant subspaces**, which cause transients when the angle between their two eigenvectors is small — [Reuter 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.05931)
- Data-driven Koopman–Schur decomposition (Drmač & Mezić): replaces ill-conditioned DMD eigenvectors with "a flag of invariant subspaces" computed by partial ordered (real) Schur decomposition with orthonormal bases — [Drmač & Mezić 2024](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2312.15837)
- Pseudospectra as the general tool for non-normal operators: Trefethen & Embree, *Spectra and Pseudospectra* (Princeton UP 2005) (†) — [Trefethen & Embree 2005](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691119465/spectra-and-pseudospectra)
- Transformer-specific [preprint]: causal attention matrices are row-stochastic and "non-normal by construction". On depth products, "eigenvalue predictions of surviving deviations err by seven to eleven orders of magnitude"; resolvent/pseudospectral features were needed — [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09279)
- Projection ambiguity [preprint]: a scalar or low-dimensional record "may reflect spectral loss of stability, hidden-state memory, nonnormal amplification … or a combination". A non-normal family can produce diverging projected variance with a fixed stable spectrum — [Herrera-Marín 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16235)
- **Random-matrix baselines for "how much rotation to expect"**: for an n×n real Gaussian (Ginibre) matrix, P(all eigenvalues real) = 2^{−n(n−1)/4} (Edelman 1997). For n = 3 this is 2^{−3/2} ≈ 0.354, so P(one real eigenvalue + a complex pair) ≈ 0.646. The expected number of real eigenvalues is √(2n/π) + O(1) (Edelman, Kostlan & Shub 1994, JAMS 7:247) — [Edelman 1997](https://math.mit.edu/~edelman/publications/probability_random.pdf); [Edelman, Kostlan & Shub 1994](https://math.mit.edu/~edelman/publications/how_many.pdf); [restated in arXiv 1512.01449](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.01449). (The n = 3 arithmetic is mine, from the published formula.)

### Inferences
- **Null for the full-192D Jacobian.** With n = 192, √(2·192/π) ≈ 11.1, so an i.i.d. Gaussian Jacobian has ~11–12 real eigenvalues, i.e., **~94% complex**. The observed 87–93% complex at initialization is therefore *at or slightly below* the generic random-matrix value. Complex eigenvalues in 192D are the default for any unstructured asymmetric matrix and are not evidence of learned rotation. The slight deficit suggests a somewhat enhanced symmetric component. (Adding the identity from the residual connection shifts eigenvalues but does not change real vs complex.)
- **Null for random 3D slices.** If J is Ginibre-like (i.i.d. Gaussian, orthogonally invariant), its compression VᵀJV onto any fixed orthonormal 3-frame has *exactly* i.i.d. Gaussian 3×3 entries. A random slice should then show a complex pair (vortex/spiral) with probability **1 − 2^{−3/2} ≈ 64.6%**. Limits: a symmetric J gives 0% vortices in every slice, and a purely antisymmetric J gives 100% (centres). The observed **41–59%** vortices thus mostly measure the **antisymmetric-to-symmetric balance of J in the slice** and indicate *more strain than a Ginibre matrix*, not a 192D spectral type. Recommended report: ‖A‖_F/‖S‖_F (with J = S + A, symmetric + antisymmetric), alongside the slice vortex fraction against the 64.6% baseline.
- **Slice-dependence of "repelling" traps.** By Embree's theorem, a slice can show Re θ > 0 whenever H = (J+Jᵀ)/2 has positive eigenvalues, even if all 192 eigenvalues of J are stable. For a Ginibre matrix normalized to unit spectral radius, H is GOE-like with spectral edge ≈ √2, so ω(J) ≈ √2 > α(J) ≈ 1 (my derivation). "Repelling" labels in slices therefore partly reflect non-normality (transient amplification directions) and need not mean instability.
- **Safer 3D views of a trap.** (i) Use real Schur vectors of J at a *true* 192D equilibrium, ordered to put the slowest eigenvalues or the largest-|Im λ| pair first. The first 2 Schur vectors span the exact rotation plane of that complex pair, and the third adds the most relevant real mode. (ii) Report the off-diagonal Schur coupling ‖N‖ (shear/feedforward) and Henrici's departure from normality, ‖J‖_F² − Σ|λᵢ|². (iii) Show ε-pseudospectra, not just eigenvalues.

### Gaps
- Goldman's derivation is confirmed only at the abstract/summary level. The search did not confirm whether he uses the Schur form explicitly (Hennequin 2012 and Murphy & Miller use the triangular/Schur picture).
- No source gives the distribution of Ritz-value types for *structured* (non-Ginibre) Jacobians such as transformer MLP Jacobians. The 64.6% baseline applies only to orthogonally invariant ensembles.
- I found no source for the exact constant in E_n beyond "√(2n/π) + O(1)".

## 4. Molecular dynamics: PCA cosines from random diffusion (Hess), TICA, VAMP/VAMPnets, MSMs, slow feature analysis

### Takeaway
PCA of a finite, unconverged trajectory of a diffusive system returns **cosine-shaped principal components with a 1/k² variance spectrum**, i.e., the same structure as pure Brownian motion. Hess's "cosine content" flags non-convergence (it cannot prove convergence). TICA (and SFA) target *slow* rather than high-variance coordinates, but TICA also yields cosines on random walks, changes abruptly with lag time, and assumes detailed balance, which erases rotational (non-reversible) structure. VAMP drops the reversibility assumption. Apparent clusters in PCA/TICA projections can arise from pure random walks.

### Cited Findings
- *Hess 2000* (Phys Rev E 62:8438) showed that the principal components of high-dimensional random diffusion are cosines, "with the number of periods equal to half the principal component index". The first PCs of protein MD resemble them (†; definition corroborated by secondary sources) — [Hess 2000](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.62.8438); [RSC supplementary describing cosine content](https://www.rsc.org/suppdata/c5/cp/c5cp06024a/c5cp06024a1.pdf)
- *Hess 2002* (Phys Rev E 65:031910) introduced **cosine content** c_i ∈ [0, 1] of the i-th PC projection p_i(t). c_i ≈ 1 means a perfect cosine (random-diffusion-like). Hess: "When the cosine content is close to 1, one can be sure that the simulation is not converged" (quoted on the GROMACS list). Low cosine content does **not** prove convergence: a Good-Turing study reports cosine contents 0.0333, 0.0040, 0.0291 and 0.0032 for the first four PCs of an unconverged trajectory, "falsely indicating … convergence". Error bars on c_i are large (for 1 ns HPr trajectories, 90% of values fell between 0.1 and 0.8) — [Hess 2002](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.65.031910); [GROMACS list](https://mailman-1.sys.kth.se/pipermail/gromacs.org_gmx-users/2006-February/019863.html); [Good-Turing paper, arXiv 1309.1108](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1309.1108)
- The definition, c_i = (2/T)(∫cos(iπt/T) p_i(t)dt)² / ∫p_i(t)²dt (†, as implemented in GROMACS `gmx analyze -cc`) — [Hess 2002](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.65.031910)
- *Palma & Pierdominici-Sottile 2024* (ACS Omega): "nonsense correlations" between independent random-walk components (random walks in harmonic potentials) make PCA of **a single trajectory** misleading. They corrupt eigenvectors and eigenvalues and even the definition of the "essential space", and produce "artificial structures" in PC probability distributions that do not match the potential. The fix is PCA on many concatenated independent simulations — [Palma & Pierdominici-Sottile 2024](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsomega.4c01515)
- **TICA**: Molgedey & Schuster 1994 (PRL 72:3634) (†) introduced time-lagged decorrelation — [Molgedey & Schuster 1994](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.72.3634). TICA was brought to MD as the identification of slow order parameters for Markov models — [Pérez-Hernández et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4811489); [Schwantes & Pande 2013 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1021/ct300878a). TICA solves C(τ)v = λC(0)v, with eigenvalues λ = e^{−τ/t_i} giving implied timescales (†)
- *Schultze & Grubmüller 2021* (JCTC 17:5766): averaged over 20,000 random walks (n = 1000 steps, d = 50), the TICA projections at short lag are ≈ cos(πt/n) and cos(2πt/n). Projections **change non-continuously with lag τ** because eigenvalue curves cross. For d = n, the eigenvalues are λ_k = cos(2πτk/n). The first transition lags scale roughly as τ ∝ n/√d (not proven). Results depend on the symmetrization used. Comparison: a 1 µs MD trajectory of the 330-residue 11AS protein had TICA projections "strikingly similar" to a **40-dimensional random walk**, while the 34-residue WW domain did not. Random-walk projections also show clusters, so "clusters in the tICA-projections … do not necessarily reflect local minima in the free energy landscape" — [Schultze & Grubmüller 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8444338/) (JCTC DOI 10.1021/acs.jctc.1c00273)
- Contrasting view: a later review argues that Schultze & Grubmüller "disregarded any discussion surrounding the choice of input features", and that the gaps could reflect poor features or sampling — [arXiv 2112.03202](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.03202)
- **Detailed balance**: TICA and similar "dynamical" reductions "are only valid if the dynamics obeys detailed balance (microscopic reversibility) and typically require long, equilibrated" trajectories. VAMP extends this to non-equilibrium dynamics (single-file ion diffusion; KcsA under an electrochemical potential), and the authors "recommend VAMP as a replacement for the less general TICA method" — [Paul et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.5083627)
- **VAMP** (Wu & Noé 2020, J Nonlinear Sci 30:23): a variational principle for approximating the Koopman operator's *singular* functions, valid for non-reversible and non-stationary processes — [Wu & Noé 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00332-019-09567-y). **VAMPnets**: neural networks trained on the VAMP score, mapping configurations to soft metastable states end to end — [Mardt et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-017-02388-1)
- TICA, DMD, EDMD, the variational approach and VAMP are unified as transfer/Koopman operator approximations (†) — [Klus et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00332-017-9437-7)
- **MSMs**: validated by implied-timescale convergence with lag and by Chapman–Kolmogorov tests (†) — [Prinz et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3565032)
- **SFA** (Wiskott & Sejnowski 2002, Neural Comput 14:715): the slowest-varying (unit-variance, decorrelated) features. Equivalent to TICA at lag 1 in the linear case (†) — [Wiskott & Sejnowski 2002](https://doi.org/10.1162/089976602317318938)

### Inferences
- Beam directions diffusing on S¹⁹¹ (angle ∝ lag^0.4) and token positions drifting across 26 checkpoints are both random-walk-like. Before interpreting any PCA/TICA view of checkpoint trajectories, compute **cosine content** of each PC across the 26 checkpoints and compare with a matched random walk (same dimension, same step-size profile). High cosine content → the "motion" seen is the generic random-walk shape.
- TICA on 26 time points in 192D (d > n) is ill-posed without strong regularization. Schultze's τ ∝ n/√d scaling suggests that projections already change abruptly for τ of order 26/√192 ≈ 2 checkpoints. TICA/SFA are therefore unreliable here unless the dimension is reduced first.
- Training is non-equilibrium (learning-rate schedule, drift), so detailed balance does not hold. TICA's symmetrization would remove the antisymmetric (rotational) part of the lagged covariance. If rotation over training is the target, use non-symmetric estimators (VAMP singular functions for slow subspaces, DMD/Koopman for oscillatory modes; Q5) or the antisymmetric part of C(τ) directly.

- I could not access Hess 2000/2002 full text this session. The exact cosine-content formula and Hess's numerical examples come from background knowledge and secondary sources.
- No study found that applies cosine content to deep-network checkpoint trajectories.

## 5. Fluid dynamics and Koopman: DMD, Koopman/EDMD, and what they reveal about rotating modes

### Takeaway
DMD/Koopman methods fit a **linear propagator** to snapshot pairs and return complex eigenvalues, i.e., explicit frequencies and growth rates for oscillatory or rotating modes. This makes them the natural tool when rotation is the question. Their failure modes are well documented: noise-biased eigenvalues, spurious modes when the observable subspace is not invariant, ill-conditioned eigenvectors (non-normality), and an inability to capture standing waves or translation without augmentation. Verified variants exist: ResDMD residuals, principal-angle invariance tests, and Schur-based modes.

### Cited Findings
- *Schmid 2010* (JFM 656:5): DMD extracts dynamic modes and eigenvalues from snapshot sequences of numerical and experimental flows — [Schmid 2010](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022112010001217). Rowley et al. 2009 (JFM 641:115) linked DMD modes to Koopman modes (†) — [Rowley et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0022112009992059)
- *Tu et al. 2014* (exact DMD): DMD as a least-squares linear operator on snapshot pairs, with modes as eigenvectors. A standing wave in a single real observable needs data augmentation/delay embedding, because one real snapshot mode cannot represent a complex-conjugate oscillation pair (†) — [Tu et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.3934/jcd.2014.1.391); textbook: [Kutz et al. 2016 (†)](https://doi.org/10.1137/1.9781611974508)
- *Williams, Kevrekidis & Rowley 2015* (EDMD): Koopman approximation on a dictionary of observables — [Williams et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00332-015-9258-5). Review: "obtaining finite-dimensional coordinate systems … in which the dynamics appear approximately linear remains a central open challenge" — [Brunton et al. 2022, SIAM Rev](https://doi.org/10.1137/21M1401243)
- **Noise bias**: sensor noise systematically biases DMD eigenvalues (typically spurious damping). Corrections include forward–backward and total-least-squares DMD — [Dawson et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00348-016-2127-7); [Hemati et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00162-017-0432-2)
- **Spurious modes and verification**: ResDMD computes residuals against the full infinite-dimensional Koopman operator, so it can reject spurious (unphysical) modes and compute pseudospectra with error control — [Colbrook & Townsend 2024, CPAM](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpa.22125); [Colbrook, Ayton & Szőke 2023, JFM](https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2022.1052). For stochastic systems, "it is necessary to go beyond expectations": add variance terms and use variance-pseudospectra to gauge "statistical coherency" (applied to mouse neural recordings) — [Colbrook et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11071-023-09135-w)
- **Invariance via principal angles** [preprint]: "finite-dimensional feature spaces … are rarely invariant, so closure failures and projection errors lead to spurious eigenvalues, misleading Koopman modes". Koopman invariance is quantified "using principal angles between a subspace and its Koopman image" (principal angle decomposition, PAD) — [Conradie et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2603.15091)
- **Ill-conditioned eigenvectors** → use Koopman–Schur (orthonormal invariant-subspace flags) — [Drmač & Mezić 2024](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2312.15837)
- **SPOD vs DMD**: spectral POD yields energy-optimal modes per frequency for statistically stationary flows, and its relation to DMD and resolvent analysis is derived — [Towne, Schmidt & Colonius 2018](https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2018.283)
- **Evaluating time-dependent projections**: 9 dimensionality-reduction methods × 10 datasets × 12 spatial/temporal-stability metrics were benchmarked for projecting time-dependent multivariate data. Visual quality trades off against temporal stability — [Vernier et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.13977)

### Inferences
- For "rotating modes" in the token cloud *across training*, DMD on the 26 checkpoint snapshots gives explicit complex eigenvalues (rotation angle per checkpoint), which jPCA approximates only within the top-6 PCs. With 26 snapshots, DMD can resolve at most ~25 modes, and noise bias plus the random-walk null (Q4/Q7) can produce *spurious* low-frequency oscillatory modes. A random walk's PCs are cosines, which DMD will happily fit as oscillations. Use ResDMD residuals or a random-walk/TME surrogate as the null.
- For the *within-frame* field (the force field over token positions), the Koopman/DMD analogue is the 192×192 Jacobian. Rotating modes are the complex Schur blocks, and the Koopman–Schur and principal-angle tools make the "is my 3D subspace invariant?" question testable. Measure the principal angles between span(V) and span(JV): small angles mean the slice is nearly invariant and its 3×3 classification is trustworthy.

### Gaps
- I found no fluid-dynamics study that quantifies how often 3D *slices* of a higher-dimensional state space misclassify critical-point type. The closest analogues are frame-dependence (Haller) and Böttcher's loss-landscape projection result (Q7).

## 6. Mori–Zwanzig: projected dynamics become non-Markovian (memory + noise); implications for "temperature" and "forces" in a projected view

### Takeaway
Projecting exact dynamics onto a few resolved coordinates gives the **generalized Langevin equation**: a Markovian (mean-force) term, a memory integral over the past, and an "orthogonal" noise term linked to the memory kernel by a fluctuation–dissipation relation. A 3D view of a 192D flow therefore shows *conditional-mean* forces plus memory and pseudo-noise generated by the 189 hidden dimensions. Apparent "temperature" (fluctuation strength) and "forces" in the projection depend on the subspace and are not intrinsic properties of the particles.

### Cited Findings
- Zwanzig 1961 (Phys Rev 124:983) and Mori 1965 (Prog Theor Phys 33:423) derived projection-operator equations for resolved variables, with memory kernels and fluctuating forces (†) — [Zwanzig 1961](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.124.983); [Mori 1965](https://doi.org/10.1143/PTP.33.423). Chorin, Hald & Kupferman 2000 (PNAS 97:2968) recast MZ for "optimal prediction": with a nonlinear (conditional-expectation) projection, the reduced equation is a Markovian conditional-mean term + memory + noise (†) — [Chorin et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.97.7.2968)
- Lin & Lu 2021 (J Comput Phys): data-driven model reduction via Wiener projections, connected to the Koopman–Mori–Zwanzig formalism — [Lin & Lu 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcp.2020.109864)
- Lin et al. 2021 (SIADS): MZ is "a natural generalization of the Koopman description". Data-driven algorithms extract the **Markov and memory kernels** from time series of a reduced set of observables (Lorenz '96). They numerically verified the generalized fluctuation–dissipation relation linking the memory kernel to the orthogonal dynamics' two-time correlations, and found the GLE better at predicting the reduced observables than approximate Koopman operators — [Lin et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1137/21M1401759)
- Lange & Grubmüller 2006 (J Chem Phys 124:214903): a GLE in a few collective (PCA-derived) coordinates of a peptide (neurotensin), with **memory kernels extracted from 1 ns of MD**. It reproduced a transition rate consistent with a 150 ns reference (transition time ~50 ns), and kernel regularization was needed — [Lange & Grubmüller 2006](https://www.em.mpg.de/634294/paper_collective_langevin_lange_jcp_2006.pdf)
- SGD does not reach equilibrium: in the late phase, the joint position–velocity stationary distribution "is in broken detailed balance, with nonzero probability currents", which drive phase-space oscillations in Hessian-eigenvector planes — [Kunin et al. 2024 (arXiv v4)](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.09133v4). SGD's most likely trajectories near critical points resemble closed loops rather than Brownian motion (non-equilibrium; †) — [Chaudhari & Soatto 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.11029)

### Inferences
- In the colloid/laser picture, the net force on a token projected into a 3D view is (i) the conditional mean of the full 192D force given the 3D coordinates, plus (ii) a memory term (hidden coordinates relax on their own timescales and feed back), plus (iii) orthogonal "noise". A "temperature" read off token jitter in 3D therefore measures **the hidden dimensions' contribution**, not an intrinsic thermal bath. It will change with the slice, and its relation to dissipation (fluctuation–dissipation) holds only if the hidden dynamics are fast and equilibrated, which a non-equilibrium training process does not guarantee (Kunin: broken detailed balance).
- Practical test, borrowed from MSMs: check Markovianity of the projected dynamics by estimating a projected linear propagator at several lags τ and testing Chapman–Kolmogorov consistency (K(2τ) ≈ K(τ)²) or implied-timescale plateaus. Failure quantifies how much memory the 3D view hides.
- Across checkpoints, the "force" on a beam (MLP neuron direction) in a 2D/3D view likewise mixes gradient components from hidden directions. Projections of SGD dynamics onto a few PCs should not be read as gradient flow on a projected potential.

## 7. Deep-learning training trajectories: PCA of random walks (Antognini & Sohl-Dickstein), Lorch 2016, loss landscapes (Li et al. 2018), linear interpolation (Goodfellow et al. 2015), tiny subspaces (Gur-Ari et al. 2018); safe projection of weight/representation trajectories

### Takeaway
For a high-dimensional random walk, PCA puts ~60% of the variance in PC1 and ~80% in PC1+PC2, with cosine components and Lissajous-curve projections. "Most variance of the training trajectory lies in two PCs" (40–90% in Li et al.) is therefore what pure diffusion also produces. Random 2D directions are nearly orthogonal to the path and show the Hessian *trace*, hiding saddles. The "tiny dominant Hessian subspace" that gradients align with mostly carries oscillation, not learning. Safe practice: compare every trajectory projection against random-walk/OU nulls, project onto Hessian or trajectory-derived directions rather than random ones, and report the variance captured.

### Cited Findings
- *Antognini & Sohl-Dickstein 2018* (NeurIPS): for a random walk in the n → ∞ limit, the variance fraction of PC k is **ρ_k = 6/(π²k²)**, which gives ~60% (PC1), ~80% (PC1–2), ~95% (first 12) and ~99% (first 66). The PC-k projection is √(2λ_k/n)·cos(πkt/n), so projections onto any two PCs are **Lissajous curves**. For an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck walk x_t = (1−α)x_{t−1} + ξ_t, λ_k ≈ [4π²k²(1−α)/n² + α²]⁻¹. In high dimension the walk is not mean-reverting but "trapped at a fixed distance" r_c = 1/√(α(2−α)) from the minimum, reached after ~(α(2−α))⁻¹ steps. Real training: ResNet-50-v2/ImageNet (after random projection to 30,000 dimensions) has a random-walk-like spectrum except for an excess PC1 (drift) and strongly random-walk-like mid-training projections; a linear CIFAR-10 model is OU-like. Caveat stated: smooth Lissajous-like PCA curves arise from noise alone — [Antognini & Sohl-Dickstein 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.08805); [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1806.08805)
- *Li et al. 2018* (NeurIPS): random directions fail for trajectories because two Gaussian random vectors in n dimensions have expected cosine ≈ √(2/(πn)). They use PCA of [θ₀−θ_n; …; θ_{n−1}−θ_n], where "40% to 90% of the variation in the descent paths lies in just two dimensions". Filter normalization rescales each random-direction filter to the norm of the corresponding trained filter, which removes scale-invariance artifacts. Caveat: the curvature of a random projection is a weighted average of Hessian eigenvalues, so apparent convexity can hide non-convexity (the converse is safe) — [Li et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.09913)
- *Böttcher & Wheeler 2024* (J Stat Mech): for two Gaussian random directions at a critical point, E[projected Hessian] = tr(H)·I₂, so the projected principal curvature is proportional to the **mean curvature** (normalized trace). Saddles in the full space "are rarely correctly identified" in random projections. In their examples, a balanced saddle (500+/500−) appears as a saddle in ~70% of projections, an 800+/500− saddle in 0 of 10,000, and a 1000+/900− saddle in ~0.5%. ResNet-56 (855,770 parameters; extreme eigenvalues −16.4 and 5007.9) looks like a minimum in random projections and a saddle along Hessian directions. Recommendation: project along dominant Hessian eigenvectors (largest positive and most negative curvature) — [Böttcher & Wheeler 2024](https://doi.org/10.1088/1742-5468/ad13fc)
- *Goodfellow, Vinyals & Saxe 2015* (ICLR): the 1D linear path from initialization to solution shows a monotonically decreasing loss with no barriers, for many architectures (†) — [Goodfellow et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6544)
- *Gur-Ari, Roberts & Dyer 2018*: after a short initial phase, the gradient lies mostly in the subspace of the top Hessian eigenvectors, whose number is about the number of classes (†) — [Gur-Ari et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.04754)
- *Song, Ahn & Yun 2025* (ICLR; ICML-2024 workshop): this alignment is "spurious". Projecting SGD updates onto the dominant subspace does not further reduce training loss, while removing the dominant component works about as well as the full update even though it strips out most of the magnitude. The same holds at the Edge of Stability, with SAM, momentum and adaptive optimizers — [Song et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.16002v1); [ICLR 2025 poster](https://iclr.cc/virtual/2025/poster/27933)
- *Kunin et al. 2024* (Neural Comput; arXiv 2107.09133): after performance plateaus, parameters keep moving with **anomalous diffusion**, ‖Δ_k‖² ∝ k^c, where c = 1 is Brownian, c > 1 super- and c < 1 sub-diffusive. Larger learning rates produce underdamped oscillations that *reduce* c; momentum's effect tracks the damping ratio, and batch size acts non-monotonically. Probability currents (broken detailed balance) drive phase-space oscillations in Hessian-eigenvector planes — [Kunin et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.09133v4). Earlier: the distance of weights from initialization grows ~logarithmically with steps ("ultra-slow diffusion") (†) — [Hoffer et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.08741)
- Lorch 2016 ("Visualizing deep network training trajectories with PCA", ICML Workshop on Visualization for Deep Learning) is widely cited as the first PCA-of-parameters visualization. I could **not** retrieve it this session; Antognini & Sohl-Dickstein describe the technique generically — see Gaps

### Inferences
- **Beam diffusion.** The observed "angle ∝ lag^0.4" is the angular analogue of Kunin's displacement law. If the angle is an RMS displacement, then MSD ∝ lag^0.8, i.e., **mildly sub-diffusive (c ≈ 0.8)**, consistent with late-phase SGD/OU-like confinement on a sphere of fixed radius (Antognini's r_c picture). A Brownian null on S¹⁹¹ would give exponent 0.5 on angle (for small angles).
- **PCA of 26 checkpoints.** The random-walk null predicts PC1 ≈ 60% and PC1+2 ≈ 80% (exact only for n → ∞; with n = 26 slightly different), plus the "cos(πt/26), cos(2πt/26)" horseshoe/parabola in the PC1–PC2 plane. Any animation of token clouds or beam directions over checkpoints in a PCA basis fitted to the trajectory will look like this whether or not structured learning dynamics exist. Report the observed variance spectrum against 6/(π²k²) and against a fitted OU spectrum.
- **3D slices of the token field.** By the √(2/(πn)) result, a random 3D slice in 192D captures on average only 3/192 ≈ 1.6% of the squared norm of any fixed direction (my arithmetic). Random slices are therefore nearly blind to the specific directions that carry the dynamics (dominant beams, slow modes). By Böttcher's argument, random slices also average curvature or strain into a trace-like quantity that hides saddles.

## 8. Transformers as interacting particle systems: Lu et al. 2019; Geshkovski, Letrouit, Polyanskiy & Rigollet (2023/2025); how token dynamics are visualized

### Takeaway
The mathematical picture has three ingredients: tokens are particles on the unit sphere (LayerNorm/RMSNorm), self-attention is an interaction kernel with inverse temperature β, and the MLP is a per-particle convection/force term. Its main prediction is **clustering**: a fast phase of cluster formation followed by a long metastable phase of slow merging. On real high-dimensional models these papers do **not** use 2D/3D projections of tokens. They plot **histograms of pairwise inner products ⟨xᵢ, xⱼ⟩ across layers**, a rotation-invariant, projection-free statistic. Sphere plots are reserved for toy models with d = 2 or 3.

### Cited Findings
- Lu et al. 2019 interpret a Transformer layer as a numerical (Lie–Trotter splitting) step for a multi-particle convection–diffusion ODE: attention is the interaction/diffusion term between particles and the FFN is the per-particle convection term. Using Strang splitting gives the "Macaron" layer (†) — [Lu et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02762)
- Geshkovski, Letrouit, Polyanskiy & Rigollet, "A mathematical perspective on Transformers", **Bull. AMS 62 (2025) 427–479** (arXiv 2312.10794). Tokens evolve on S^{d−1} because of layer normalization, with simplified Q = K = V = I attention dynamics and β an inverse temperature. There is a mean-field continuity-equation view, and a surrogate model that is a Wasserstein gradient flow of an interaction energy. Theorems: convergence to a single point for almost every initialization when β = 0, small β (≲ n⁻¹) or large β (≳ n²); for d ≥ 3, any β, convergence to one cluster; exponential rate when d ≥ n. They observe **metastability**, a long intermediate phase with a few clusters that merge slowly, which they "cannot yet explain theoretically". Visualization of real models: Fig. 1 shows **histograms of pairwise inner products** across layers of ALBERT XLarge v2 (tokens in ℝ⁴⁰⁹⁶, sequence ~512–1024, one Wikipedia paragraph). Mass near 1 grows with depth, and more so with 48 layers — [Geshkovski et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10794); [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2312.10794)
- Geshkovski et al. 2023 (NeurIPS), "The emergence of clusters in self-attention dynamics": with time-independent weights, tokens cluster toward limiting objects determined by the initial tokens. The **type of limiting object depends on the spectrum of the value matrix V**. In 1D, the attention matrix converges to a low-rank Boolean matrix ("leaders") — [Geshkovski et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05465)
- Attention operators are non-normal, so eigenvalue reasoning fails at finite depth [preprint] — [Li 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09279)

### Inferences
- For a ViT with d = 192 and 600 tokens, the projection-free equivalents are: (i) histograms of cos(xᵢ, xⱼ) per layer and per checkpoint (the ALBERT-style plot); (ii) the eigenvalue spectrum of the 600×600 Gram matrix (cluster count ≈ number of large eigenvalues); (iii) the spectrum of the value/output matrices, which by Geshkovski 2023 determines cluster geometry. All are invariant to rotations of the residual basis, so they avoid the slice-dependence problem entirely.
- The "colloids under lasers" metaphor matches these models closely. Within a frame, attention is the pairwise interaction and the MLP "beams" are the per-token force field. The theory predicts metastable cluster phases, which in a 3D slice could appear as attracting "traps". Whether a trap is a true metastable cluster should be checked in the full space (pairwise inner products, cluster persistence across layers), not in a slice.

- Lu et al. 2019 was not re-read this session. No paper found that visualizes *training-time* (checkpoint) evolution of token clusters in this framework.

## 9. Comparing subspaces and dynamics over time: principal angles, subspace alignment, Procrustes, CCA

### Takeaway
To compare views across checkpoints: use **principal angles** (Björck–Golub) for "how much did the subspace rotate", orthogonal **Procrustes** to remove a rigid rotation before comparing configurations, and **CCA** to align latent dynamics when bases are arbitrary (Gallego). Then compare the **dynamics within** the aligned subspaces: fixed-point topology, propagator spectra, or DSA-type Procrustes over vector fields. Geometry-similarity scores (SVCCA/CKA) alone can rank an untrained network as more similar to a trained one than two trained networks are to each other.

### Cited Findings
- Principal angles θ₁ ≤ … ≤ θ_k between two subspaces with orthonormal bases Q_A, Q_B are the arccos of the singular values of Q_AᵀQ_B (numerically stable SVD algorithm; Björck & Golub 1973, Math Comp 27:579) (†) — [Björck & Golub 1973](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0025-5718-1973-0348991-3)
- Orthogonal Procrustes: min_R ‖A − BR‖_F over orthogonal R is solved by the SVD of BᵀA (Schönemann 1966, Psychometrika 31:1) (†) — [Schönemann 1966](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02289451). Generalized shape metrics (Procrustes-based) give proper metrics between neural representations (†) — [Williams et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.14739)
- Dynamical Similarity Analysis compares *dynamics* rather than geometry: fit linear operators (DMD on delay embeddings) and compare them up to orthogonal similarity transforms, i.e., a Procrustes over vector fields (†) — [Ostrow et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.10168)
- CCA alignment of per-session PCA manifolds: aligned 0.93 vs unaligned 0.38 normalized similarity, stable over up to ~2 years. Static-cluster alignment and tuning-based simulations align worse, so temporal structure matters — [Gallego et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-019-0555-4)
- SVCCA/CKA geometry differs by architecture while fixed-point topology is shared. SVCCA rated trained tanh vs trained ReLU at 0.72 but trained tanh vs untrained at ~0.80–0.81 — [Maheswaranathan et al. 2019b](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.08549)
- Principal angles between a subspace and its image under the propagator certify Koopman invariance [preprint] — [Conradie et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2603.15091)
- Time-dependent projection methods trade visual quality against temporal stability (9 methods, 12 metrics) — [Vernier et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.13977)

### Inferences
- **Beams.** The 66° drift over 140 epochs is a per-vector angle. For *sets* of beams, compute principal angles between the top-k subspace spanned by the 768 beam directions (or by the token cloud) at checkpoints t and t+Δ, and fit the same lag scaling (θ ∝ Δ^γ) to the leading principal angle. Compare with the null for random k-subspaces of ℝ¹⁹² (principal angles near 90° unless k is large) and with a matched spherical random walk.
- **Animating checkpoints.** Even if the residual-stream basis is not fully gauge-free (LayerNorm gains, embeddings and readout break exact rotation invariance), a Procrustes alignment between consecutive checkpoints' token clouds separates rigid rotation of the whole cloud (a "coordinate drift" with no functional meaning) from genuine deformation. Present both the Procrustes rotation angle and the residual after alignment.
- **Which subspaces show dynamics faithfully: a decision checklist** synthesized from Q1–Q9.
  1. *Rotation/vortex questions*: the real Schur plane of the complex pair of interest at a true 192D fixed point (exact for that mode), or DMD/Koopman modes across checkpoints with ResDMD/random-walk nulls. Not random slices (their baseline is 64.6% vortices for unstructured matrices) and not jPCA without TME/CMPT controls.
  2. *Slow modes / metastable traps*: VAMP (non-reversible) or TICA only after dimension reduction and with lag scans. Check cosine content.
  3. *Fixed points*: find in 192D (minimize ‖F‖²), then project into top-PC space and report the fraction of the fixed points' variance captured.
  4. *Transient amplification / "repelling" labels*: numerical abscissa vs spectral abscissa, pseudospectra, and Schur coupling. Treat slice-level instability as non-normality unless it is confirmed in 192D.
  5. *Clusters*: projection-free cosine-similarity histograms and Gram spectra (Geshkovski).
  6. *Training-time motion*: always overlay the random-walk (6/(π²k²)) and OU spectra, plus a phantom-oscillation diagnostic (Shinn).
  7. *"Forces/temperature" in 3D*: test Markovianity of the projected dynamics (Chapman–Kolmogorov), and expect memory and pseudo-noise from the hidden dimensions (Mori–Zwanzig).
